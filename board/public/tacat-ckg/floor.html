02 · The floor

No finite observer separates anything at zero cost.

This is the load-bearing constant of the whole framework, and it appears in two forms that must not be confused. One is arithmetic you can read off a datasheet. The other is an epistemic claim about bounded observers. Every result on this page uses only the first.

The instrumental floor — this is all the theorems need

A monitor with N distinguishable outputs partitioning a range of diameter D has a mean cell of exactly D/N, so at least one cell is that wide or wider. It is a one-line count, and unlike an existence argument it yields a number you can compute before buying the instrument.

A correction to the usual argument. The customary form — no finite monitor returns a point, so some positive floor exists — is false as stated. A finite monitor may have cells of diameter zero: the sign-with-zero quantiser on [−1, 1] has cells of width 1, 0 and 1. The infimum of cell diameters is therefore not in general positive, and any theorem resting on that infimum is unsound. What survives is a bound on the cell you actually occupy, not on the smallest cell in the partition. This framework's own earlier statements used the unsound form.

A plate reader, worked through

An instrument reporting optical density on [0, 4] to three decimal places has N = 4001 distinguishable outputs and a mean cell of about 10⁻³ OD units. Any ranking of variants whose OD differences fall below that is a ranking of quantiser codes, not of variants.

Channels do not add resolution the way you expect

In d dimensions the linear resolution of a partition into N cells falls only as N−1/d. A monitor observing five channels at eight bits each has 2⁴⁰ states and a linear resolution corresponding to about 2⁸ per axis. The state count is enormous and almost none of it is discrimination along any one axis.

More resolution often buys exactly nothing

If the decision the instrument feeds takes finitely many distinct actions, then past the point where the monitor can realise the optimal partition, additional resolution buys nothing at all. And the bound is not merely asymptotic: resolution can be non-monotone. Adding one cell can destroy an alignment that a coarser monitor happened to have.

Triplicate protects against the wrong failure

Three independent sources are enough to survive one source that dies, and enough to survive one that fails obviously. They are not enough to survive one that drifts. A miscalibrated instrument is an adaptive fault: its output tracks the true signal with an offset, so it clusters with an honest channel and a majority rule names the wrong value.

The consequence for a bench protocol

Robustness against one arbitrarily-faulty source requires n ≥ 3f + 1 — four sources, not three. A triplicate measurement protects against a dead instrument and not against a miscalibrated one, and miscalibration is the more common failure.

What the simulation shows against what the theorem says. The classical bound gives n ≥ 4 for one arbitrary fault, and that is correct for the algorithm the theorem invokes. Plain majority voting is weaker, and the adversary simulated here is stronger than the model: under majority voting the drifting case is resolved only from five. Both facts are worth stating, and the gap between them is the difference between a voting rule and an agreement protocol.
