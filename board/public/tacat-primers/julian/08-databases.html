Chapter 8

The reference databases

Six public databases supply every identifier in this project. Which one you anchor to is not a cosmetic choice: it determines whether a federated query is possible at all, and the first submission foreclosed federation without noticing.

2. ChEBI — molecules

Chemical Entities of Biological Interest, curated at the EBI. Every distinct chemical species gets a number, and — per chapter 7 — distinct includes each protonation state and each tautomer separately. ChEBI is an OBO Foundry ontology, so entries sit in a subsumption hierarchy (L-alanine is a alpha-amino acid is a carboxylic acid) and are linked by typed relations including is_conjugate_base_of and is_tautomer_of — the exact relationships that caused the trouble. 

http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/CHEBI_29985

Note the pattern: obo/ plus PREFIX_number, with an underscore in the IRI even though the same identifier is written CHEBI:29985 in prose. Both spellings appear constantly and a string comparison between them fails.

3. Rhea — reactions, and two facts that cost time

Rhea, at the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, is the reference set of biochemical reactions. It is what UniProt uses to say what an enzyme does, which makes it the natural anchor for anything that wants to talk to protein data.

Fact one: four identifiers per reaction

Every Rhea reaction has a master plus three directional children at consecutive offsets:

IDIsCited by
19453master — undirectedUniProt’s Reaction= line, always
19454left → rightPhysiologicalDirection=
19455right → leftPhysiologicalDirection=
19456bidirectional—

Chapter 6 showed the payoff: E. coli AlaA and human ALT1 share master 19453 and differ on the child. Build on the master, then treat direction as separate information — conflating the two corrupts the graph in a way that looks fine.

Fact two: Rhea models reactions as OWL classes

This is a genuine trap, and it costs an afternoon if you hit it cold:

What you would write 
?rhea a rh:Reaction .

Returns zero rows. No error, no warning, no hint.

What is correct 
?rhea rdfs:subClassOf rh:Reaction .

A Rhea reaction is a class of chemical events, not an individual event. So the relationship is subsumption, not instantiation.

Worth noticing that this is a modelling decision Rhea made and this project made differently — tacat models its reactions as individuals. Both are defensible; the reactions here are specific curated records rather than universals. It is the kind of divergence worth being able to name.

4. UniProt — proteins, and the EC hierarchy for free

UniProt is the protein sequence and annotation database. Two parts matter here.

Accessions like P24298 identify a specific protein in a specific organism. Reviewed entries (Swiss-Prot) are manually curated; unreviewed (TrEMBL) are automatic — which is why the federated query in chapter 2 filters on up:reviewed true. Without that filter the counts inflate by orders of magnitude with machine predictions.

Enzyme class IRIs are the more interesting part. There is no OBO ontology for EC numbers, so the community convention is UniProt’s: 

http://purl.uniprot.org/enzyme/2.6.1.2

That is a real up:Enzyme OWL class with the four-level EC hierarchy from chapter 6 materialised as rdfs:subClassOf. So “find me every transaminase” is one property path rather than string surgery on a dotted number: 

?enzyme rdfs:subClassOf+ <http://purl.uniprot.org/enzyme/2.6.1> .

Rhea itself emits ec triples pointing at these same purl.uniprot.org/enzyme/ IRIs, which independently confirms the choice — you are using the identifier the ecosystem uses.

The relationship, precisely

An EC class is an activity; a UniProt accession is a protein that has that activity. Related, not identical — so the link is UniProt’s own up:enzyme, written in UniProt’s direction: <protein> up:enzyme <ec-class>. The evidence is right there in the data: P17174 carries both 2.6.1.1 and 2.6.1.3, so any claim of identity would be saying one protein is two different activities. Using UniProt’s property also makes the link checkable against its source, and a test does exactly that.

5. SBO — roles as identifiers rather than strings

The Systems Biology Ontology supplies controlled terms for the roles in the bridge node, so “reactant” is a URL rather than a free-text string that can be typo’d or translated:

SBO termRole
SBO_0000010reactant
SBO_0000011product
SBO_0000013catalyst
One easy mistake, flagged in the shapes file

SBO:0000014 is “enzyme” — but it lives in SBO’s physical-entity branch, not its role branch. Using it as a participant role is a common error, because the name reads as though it belongs. The SHACL shape restricts roles to reactant and product with sh:in and says why in its sh:message.

6. Why KEGG was dropped, and what it cost

KEGG is the best-known reaction database and the obvious first choice. The first submission anchored on it. Two consequences followed, and the second is the serious one:

1

KEGG’s ChEBI mapping returns the neutral parent

The conv/chebi cross-reference maps to the neutral form, not the physiological microspecies. That is the direct cause of the six wrong identifiers in chapter 7 — a systematic method producing a systematic error.

2

No Rhea identifier means no federation, at all

UniProt speaks Rhea. There was not a single Rhea ID anywhere in the first submission, so the join in chapter 2 was not merely unimplemented — it was impossible. The FAIR interoperability claim in the README had no route to being demonstrated.

The general lesson

Identifier choice is an architectural decision, not a data-entry detail. It determines which other systems you can ever talk to, and the cost of getting it wrong is not paid at the moment of the mistake — it is paid later, as a capability that quietly does not exist.

The nearest thing in your world: picking an ID scheme for a public API. Sequential integers versus UUIDs versus a natural key looks like a small choice on day one and dictates every integration afterwards.

7. PURLs, w3id, and the one that does not resolve

A PURL is a persistent URL: a stable address run by the curating institution, redirecting to wherever the resource currently lives. The point is that the identifier outlives the hosting arrangement. purl.obolibrary.org, purl.uniprot.org and rdf.rhea-db.org are all institutionally operated and stable.

w3id.org is the community equivalent for people who do not run an institution: a redirect service backed by a GitHub repository, where you register a namespace by pull request.

An honest gap in this project

Every locally minted IRI sits under https://w3id.org/bitspark/tacat/, and that namespace is not registered, so the IRIs do not dereference. Follow one and you get a 404.

This is not fatal — an IRI is an identifier first and a locator second, and plenty of published ontologies are in the same state. But it should be said rather than left for a reviewer to discover, and the two honest fixes are to register the namespace by PR or to serve the ontology under a path that genuinely resolves.

Opaque identifiers rather than readable ones

One convention that looks wrong to a web developer and is deliberate. The project mints tacat:REACT_0000001 with an rdfs:label, not tacat:AlanineTransaminaseReaction.

Every major vocabulary in this space does the same: OBO uses PREFIX_0000123, EMMO uses UUIDs, and NFDI4Cat’s own Voc4Cat uses w3id.org/nfdi4cat/voc4cat_0000123. The reason is that a label can be corrected, translated or re-scoped without breaking every dataset that references the term — the identifier is a key and the label is display text, and conflating them means a copy-edit is a breaking change.

You have made this trade before

Slugs versus IDs in URLs. /posts/42 survives a retitle; /posts/my-post does not, and now you own a redirect table. Same trade-off, much longer time horizon — these identifiers are expected to outlive the projects that mint them.

8. The one place this project mints anyway

Worth knowing because it is a fair challenge. “Reuse, never mint” is the slogan, but tacat does not use ChEBI PURLs as its nodes. It mints tacat:CHEBI_29985 and declares it an instance of the ChEBI class.

What tacat does 
tacat:CHEBI_29985
    a tacat:AminoAcid ,
      <http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/CHEBI_29985> ;
    rdfs:label "L-glutamate" .

Local assertions stay on a local node. The graph never appears to make claims about ChEBI’s own term.

The alternative 
<http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/CHEBI_29985>
    a tacat:AminoAcid ;
    rdfs:label "L-glutamate" .

Simpler, more obviously “reuse”, one fewer hop in every query — at the cost of asserting into somebody else’s namespace.

Both are standard practice. It is logged as an open decision (D2 in docs/GAPS_0.md), defensible either way, and the useful position is being able to argue it rather than having picked one silently.

Chapter 8 in four lines
  • ChEBI for molecules, Rhea for reactions, UniProt for proteins and EC classes, SBO for roles.
  • Rhea has four IDs per reaction and models them as OWL classes — both are silent-failure traps.
  • KEGG’s ChEBI mapping caused the identifier error, and choosing KEGG made federation impossible.
  • Identifier choice is architecture: it decides which systems you can ever talk to.
