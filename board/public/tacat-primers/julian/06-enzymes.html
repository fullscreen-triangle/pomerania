Chapter 6

Enzymes from zero

No chemistry assumed. By the end of this chapter you will understand what the three reactions in the project actually are, why one molecule that is present in all of them must not be modelled as a participant, and why “substrate” and “product” cannot be types.

1. A reaction is a rearrangement

Molecules are atoms held together by bonds. A chemical reaction breaks some bonds and makes others; the atoms are conserved, only their arrangement changes. Conventionally:

L-alanine + 2-oxoglutarate ⇌ pyruvate + L-glutamate Left of the arrow: what goes in. Right: what comes out. The double arrow means the reaction runs in both directions.

Two invariants hold and both are checkable, which makes them useful as tests:

  • Mass balance — the same count of each element on both sides. Atoms are not created.
  • Charge balance — the total electric charge is the same on both sides.

For the reaction above:

LeftRight
Formula C3H7NO2 + C5H4O5 C3H3O3 + C5H8NO4 C8H11NO7 both sides
Charge 0 + (−2) (−1) + (−1) −2 both sides
This is a property-based test

An invariant that must hold over the data regardless of which reaction you look at. The project tests it, which is an original idea nobody asked for. Chapter 7 explains the sting: the test passed on data that was wrong, because it checked an invariant of the values without ever checking that the values belonged to the identifiers written beside them.

2. What an enzyme is

Most useful reactions, left alone in water at body temperature, run far too slowly to matter. An enzyme is a protein that makes one specific reaction go fast — typically by a factor of 10⁶ to 10¹⁷ — and comes out unchanged, ready to do it again.

Three properties, all of which matter for modelling:

  • Not consumed. It is not on either side of the equation. One enzyme molecule processes thousands of substrate molecules per second.
  • Specific. It accelerates one transformation, or a narrow family, because catalysis depends on physical fit in a pocket called the active site.
  • It does not change the destination. An enzyme changes how fast equilibrium is reached, never where equilibrium lies. This is why reversibility is a property of the reaction, not of the catalyst.
If an analogy helps

A JIT compiler. It does not change what your program computes — it changes how long the computation takes, by a large constant factor, and it is still there afterwards to do it again. An enzyme is a physical object that does the same thing to a chemical transformation.

The analogy breaks in one place worth knowing: an enzyme accelerates the reaction in both directions equally. Which way material actually flows is decided by concentrations, not by the enzyme.

Substrate and product

Substrate = what goes in. Product = what comes out. Simple, and the source of the project’s central modelling problem, because these are roles in a particular reaction running in a particular direction — not properties of the molecule.

L-glutamate is a product of all three reactions here. In glutamate dehydrogenase it is a substrate. Same molecule, same graph, opposite role. Any model that types molecules as substrates or products is broken before it starts — which is exactly why participation needs the bridge node from chapter 1, carrying a role.

3. EC numbers

Enzymes are classified by the Enzyme Commission number: four integers, a strict hierarchy, each level narrowing.

LevelValueMeans
12Transferase — moves a chemical group from one molecule to another
22.6…transferring nitrogen-containing groups
32.6.1…specifically a transaminase
42.6.1.2serial number: alanine transaminase
The modelling trap in one sentence

An EC number classifies an activity, not a protein. It names a transformation that something can catalyse. The relationship between EC numbers and actual proteins is many-to-many:

  • One EC number, many proteins — human, E. coli and yeast all have an alanine transaminase, all EC 2.6.1.2, all different proteins with different sequences.
  • One protein, many EC numbers — human GOT1 (UniProt P17174) carries both 2.6.1.1 and 2.6.1.3. Cysteine transaminase activity is a secondary activity of the aspartate transaminase protein.

So “three reactions” here is not “three proteins”. The project’s reference data records this correctly and the ontology builder currently mints three separate Enzyme individuals — a known divergence between the data and the code, logged as K5 in docs/PLAN.md.

One more identifier note that pays off later: there is no OBO ontology for EC numbers, so inventing obo:EC_2_6_1_2 would be wrong. The right IRI is http://purl.uniprot.org/enzyme/2.6.1.2 — a real OWL class in UniProt with the EC hierarchy materialised as rdfs:subClassOf, so the tree in the table above is traversable with a property path.

4. What a transaminase does

A transaminase (or aminotransferase) moves an amino group — a nitrogen atom with two hydrogens, −NH2 — from one molecule to another. That is the whole job, and it is a swap rather than a one-way transfer.

Two families of molecule take part, and they trade places:

TypeHasRole going inBecomes
amino acidan −NH2 group the donora 2-oxo acid (it lost the group)
2-oxo acida C=O in the 2-position the acceptoran amino acid (it gained the group)

So each side of the equation has one of each type, and the classes swap across the arrow. That symmetry is the net-reaction pattern of a transamination — and it is exactly the four-conjunct defined class from chapter 3:

amino acid + 2-oxo acid ⇌ 2-oxo acid + amino acid consumes an amino acid · consumes a 2-oxo acid · produces a 2-oxo acid · produces an amino acid — the four conditions HermiT checks.
Now the negative control makes chemical sense

Glutamate dehydrogenase runs L-glutamate → 2-oxoglutarate — an amino acid in, a 2-oxo acid out. It contains both headline molecules of the transaminations, but it only has half the pattern: there is no 2-oxo acid consumed and no amino acid produced, because the amino group is not transferred to a partner, it is stripped off and released as ammonia.

That is a real chemical difference, and it is what the reasoner detects. The definition is not an arbitrary set of conditions that happens to exclude one thing — it encodes what makes a transamination a transamination.

The three reactions in the project

All three use the same acceptor, 2-oxoglutarate, and therefore all three make the same amino-acid product, L-glutamate. Only the donor varies:

2.6.1.2alanine transaminase (ALT) L-alaninepyruvate19453

That shared acceptor and shared product is why these three were chosen: it gives the graph a genuine cross-cutting question (which reactions involve L-glutamate?) rather than three disconnected records.

You have almost certainly seen two of these

ALT and AST are the standard liver-function markers on a blood panel. They live inside liver cells; when cells are damaged the enzymes leak into the bloodstream, so elevated ALT/AST is a damage signal. Same enzymes, same EC numbers, being measured for a completely different reason.

Why industry cares

Many drug molecules contain an amine group attached to a carbon with four different neighbours — a chiral centre, which can exist in two mirror-image forms. Usually only one form works; the other is inactive or harmful. Ordinary chemistry tends to produce a 50/50 mixture that then has to be separated, expensively.

Enzymes are chiral themselves, so they make one form and not the other. Engineered transaminases are used industrially for exactly this — the best-known case being the manufacture of the diabetes drug sitagliptin, where an evolved transaminase replaced a high-pressure metal-catalysed step.

This is the assessor’s field: LARA, his lab-automation platform, describes itself as “fully automatized transaminase high throughput protein screening.” The three reactions are not arbitrary demo data — they are his daily subject matter.

And the one that follows from it — the definition cannot classify his enzyme

The transaminases LARA screens are ω-transaminases — amine transaminases. They run amine + keto acid → ketone + amino acid. There is no amino-acid donor and no 2-oxo-acid product, so two of the four conjuncts fail and the reasoner does not classify them.

The three shipped reactions are α-transaminations, so the model fits what it models. What overclaims is the class name. Answer with the fix, not an apology: "α-transamination is what I defined. A hierarchy is the fix — α and ω as siblings under a parent requiring only the amino-group transfer — not a wider definition."

This is the most likely hard question in the whole project, precisely because the paragraph above is right that these are his daily subject matter.

5. Cofactors — present, essential, and not participants

Some enzymes cannot work with protein alone; they need a non-protein helper molecule called a cofactor. The distinction that matters for modelling:

A substrate

Consumed. Enters the reaction, is chemically transformed, leaves as something else. Appears in the equation. Counts toward mass and charge balance.

A prosthetic group

Not consumed. Bound to the enzyme, changes during the reaction, and is returned to its starting state before the next cycle. Does not appear in the equation and does not balance.

All three of these enzymes require pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP), a derivative of vitamin B6. In the resting enzyme it is bonded to an active-site lysine as an internal aldimine; during catalysis that linkage is displaced and it forms an external aldimine with the substrate. It stays with the enzyme across the cycle.

6. Ping-pong bi-bi, and why PLP does not balance

The mechanism explains the modelling decision, and it takes sixty seconds. The reaction happens in two half-reactions, and the enzyme alternates between two states.

Half 1

The donor amino acid binds. Its amino group is handed to PLP, which becomes PMP (the aminated form). The donor, now missing its amino group, leaves as a 2-oxo acid.

→ first product leaves
before second substrate binds →

Half 2

2-oxoglutarate binds. PMP hands the amino group over, becoming PLP again. The acceptor, now carrying the amino group, leaves as L-glutamate.

“Bi-bi” = two substrates in, two products out. “Ping-pong” = the first product departs before the second substrate arrives, and the cofactor bounces between two forms carrying the group across.

The consequence

PLP ends the cycle in exactly the state it started. Over one complete turnover its net change is zero. It therefore does not appear in the equation and does not enter the balance — which is why Rhea’s equations for all three reactions have exactly four participants and none of them is PLP, and why UniProt records it as a COFACTOR annotation on the protein rather than as a reactant.

So the ontology puts hasCofactor on Enzyme, never on BiochemicalReaction. PLP is a fact about the catalyst, not about the transformation.

Why this is the domain-knowledge probe

A developer given “these three reactions all need PLP” and no enzymology would model PLP as a fourth substrate. It is the obvious reading. And every syntactic check would still pass: the graph validates, SHACL is happy, queries return rows. Only the stoichiometry would be a lie — a fact nothing in the software stack can detect.

There is a test named test_plp_is_not_a_reaction_participant asserting it never appears as one. Getting this right is quiet and specific evidence of understanding the chemistry rather than only the RDF, and the first submission got it right while never saying so — leaving its strongest signal invisible.

7. Direction: the same reaction, opposite roles

Last piece, and the sharpest one. Reversible means the reaction genuinely runs both ways; which way it actually runs in a living cell depends on concentrations and on what the cell needs.

Concretely, from UniProt:

ProteinOrganismRhea masterPhysiological directionL-glutamate is…
P24298human ALT119453 left → rightproduced
P0A959E. coli AlaA19453 right → leftconsumed

Same reaction. Same identifier. Opposite roles. E. coli runs it biosynthetically to make alanine, consuming the glutamate that human ALT1 produces.

This is the experimental evidence that substrate and product cannot be intrinsic properties, and it is why the bridge node carries a role. It is also, per the design notes, the single best thirty seconds available in a presentation: a modelling decision justified by a measured biological fact rather than by an appeal to good practice.

The nuance to keep straight

Rhea gives each reaction four identifiers: a master (undirected) and three directional children. UniProt’s Reaction= line always cites the master; the PhysiologicalDirection= line cites a child. Conflating them corrupts the graph.

Which raises a fair challenge, logged as D3 in docs/GAPS_0.md: if the project links to the master, is it entitled to say a reaction “produces” L-glutamate? Verified answer: the master does carry a left side, a right side and a canonically written equation, so assigning roles per that written orientation is consistent — and the competency question is worded “in the left-to-right orientation” for exactly this reason.

Chapter 6 in five lines
  • An enzyme accelerates a reaction without being consumed, and does not change where equilibrium lies.
  • EC numbers classify activities, not proteins — the relationship is many-to-many.
  • A transaminase swaps an amino group between an amino acid and a 2-oxo acid; both classes appear on both sides.
  • PLP cycles and returns to its starting state, so it does not balance and is a property of the enzyme.
  • Direction is organism-specific, so substrate and product are roles rather than types.
