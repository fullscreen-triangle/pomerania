Four proposals, unequal in value. This chapter prices them, says which one to build first, says which one to drop, and states the case against the whole approach in the strongest form I can put it — because a proposal that has not been argued against has not been argued for.

2. The one to drop, and why

Retracting the SHACL coherence constraint for this repository

Chapter 3 proposes sh:minCount 3 on tacat:supportedBy. For a nine-entity demonstration graph, I would not ship it, and the reason is the project's own rule.

PLAN.md R5, quoted in RATIONALE.md §6: "any line that cannot be explained under questioning is a liability, not an asset. Where something cannot be defended, cut it." The constraint's defence is a theorem nobody has reviewed. Asked "why three?" in a presentation, the honest answer is because an unpublished paper of mine proves a triangle is the shortest robust support cycle — which converts a data-quality gate into a discussion of my own unrefereed work, in a submission being graded on Technologie, Qualität, Stil.

That is a bad trade at this scale. The principle — record which independent sources back each fact, and what each agrees on — is worth keeping as tacat:supportedBy without the cardinality constraint. It carries the information and costs no argument.

The constraint earns its place at curation scale, where nobody can check each entity by hand and a count is the only mechanism available. That is a different project, and saying so is more useful than defending it here.

3. The case against all of this

Put as strongly as I can put it, because this is the version that will be put to me.

The objection

Every one of these four proposals is reachable by ordinary engineering reasoning. "Check the identifier against the source." "Don't stop because the tests are green." "Corroborate." "Test that your documented limits are real." None of it needs a theory of individuation.

Dressing routine good practice in theorems imported from unpublished work is the opposite of the discipline this repository demonstrates everywhere else — where every claim is tied to a commit SHA, a DOI, or a live primary source.

What survives it

Most of it is conceded. The proposals are reachable without the theory, and that is why every chapter states them so they stand alone.

What the theory adds is not the proposals but the ordering and the prediction: it says in advance which gates cannot work, and why adding more of the same kind will not help. G10 was found by deliberate corruption after the fact. The floor result says every internal check has that blind spot, before you write it.

The honest summary

The theory earns its keep as a generator of the right question, not as justification for the answer. "What is this check comparing against, and does that thing live inside my graph?" is worth having as a reflex. Whether it arrives via a floor theorem or via scar tissue from G10 makes no difference to the code — and the code is what is being graded.
