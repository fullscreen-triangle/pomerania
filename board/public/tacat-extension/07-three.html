Chapter 7 · second series

Three verdicts on one run

A transaminase screen, twelve hours in, with one plate behaving oddly. This chapter walks the same run past three independent validations that share no subtasks and produce three unrelated answers — and argues that the reason to keep them separate is that the interesting outcome in each is the middle one, which any attempt to merge them destroys first.

★★★ the actual idea 14 min worked example the one to argue with
Provenance. The architecture below is my own unpublished design, unreviewed and unused by anyone else. The screening scenario is constructed to be plausible from public descriptions of transaminase high-throughput work — it is not taken from any real LARA run, and I have no access to one. It is a worked example chosen to make the three verdicts concrete, and the right response to it is to say where a real run would behave differently.

1. The run

A screen over a mutant library: express, harvest, lyse, assay, evaluate. Twelve hours in, plate 7 starts reporting activity values that are low but not flat. Something should decide what happens next. Today that decision is a person looking at a curve.

A single gate must turn that into continue or abort. The three validations do not answer that question. They answer three different ones, and none of them is "continue or abort".

2. What each one asks

Necessity — the orchestrator asks

Does this plate still bear on the question the campaign is asking? Its subtasks are the claims the screen is trying to support: that a given mutant has improved activity on the target amine.

On this run

Plate 7 carries eight of the twelve mutants in the region of the library the campaign actually cares about. Whatever else is true, the claim is not answerable without it. Verdict: supported — still load-bearing.

Sufficiency — the scheduler asks

Is this run still converging on completion? Its subtasks are the steps — a wholly different decomposition, in which "mutant 4B" does not appear at all and "read plate 7 at t+30" does.

On this run

The 11-minute lysis delay pushed the assay window; slope is degrading faster than the remaining reads can resolve. The measured descent toward the threshold has flattened. Verdict: stalled — not failed, not converging.

Value — purpose asks

What here is worth keeping? Its subtasks are the artefacts — lysate aliquots, raw reads, the PLP batch record. A third decomposition again.

On this run

The reads are cheap to regenerate if the plate is re-run; the lysate is consumed and is not. The PLP batch-B record is the only observation of a new lot. Verdict: mixed — keep the lysate record and the batch note, the reads are regenerable.

Notice what just happened

Three verdicts: supported, stalled, partly regenerable. They do not conflict, because they are not answers to the same question — there is no shared row to disagree on. The plate is simultaneously necessary to the campaign, not converging as scheduled, and holding one irreplaceable artefact. A single gate has to pick one of those to report and discard the other two.

3. Why merging them is the failure, not the goal

The natural engineering instinct is to combine three signals into a score and threshold it. That instinct is what Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 are both about, arriving from different directions, and it fails here for a reason that is easy to state:

Each verdict is three-valued, and the middle value is the informative one design consequence of the floor result, Ch. 1 §2

Because no finite process closes, every validation needs a third outcome that is neither success nor failure: inconclusive for necessity, stalled for sufficiency, regenerable for value. That third outcome is not a degenerate case to be minimised — it is where the residual lives, and it is the only one that tells you what to do next.

A merged score maps the three middles onto one number and they annihilate: stalled pulls it down, supported pulls it up, and the result is a middling number that reads as "marginal" — which is exactly the reading that loses the actionable content of all three.

Concretely, on plate 7, each middle verdict implies a different intervention, and they are not alternatives — all three are correct simultaneously:

VerdictWhat it says to doWhat is lost if merged
supported (necessity) Do not drop these mutants from the campaign — re-plan around them. An abort decision silently narrows the library and nobody records that it did.
stalled (sufficiency) The current schedule cannot finish this plate. Re-queue it, do not extend it. "Marginal" reads as "keep going", which spends another three hours proving it.
regenerable (value) Discard the reads freely; preserve the lysate aliquot and the batch-B record. A blanket discard destroys the only observation of the new cofactor lot.
The design constraint that follows, and it is an unusual one

If the three ever agree systematically, the architecture is wrong — it would mean they are not independent measurements but one measurement reported three times, and two of the three could be deleted. Convergence is the falsification condition, not the success condition. I know of no other part of the design where the thing that would look like validation is in fact the disproof, and it is the claim I would most want tested by someone who has built a scheduler. Chapter 8 §4 states the experiment.

4. Back to the footnote

Chapter 6 §4 quoted CoRDI 2023 footnote 2: "Modification of a running process is currently under development." The plate-7 walk-through is what that problem looks like with the three questions kept apart.

"Can this running process be modified?" has no single answer. What a modification needs to know is: which completed work is still load-bearing under the new plan (necessity), whether the modified plan can still finish from the current state (sufficiency), and which already-produced material survives the change (value). Those are the three decompositions. A system that keeps them separate can answer a modification request with three facts instead of one verdict — and three facts are what a person deciding whether to accept the modification actually needs.

5. The connection back to this submission

This is not a detour from the tacat work. It is the same argument one layer up.

Chapter 1 §1 recorded G10: nine competency questions, a SHACL shape and a type test, all green over a graph in which every external identifier had been replaced with a fabrication. Three gates, one answer each, all the same answer, all wrong. The reason they were blind was not that they were badly written — it was that they were all asking a variant of the same question, and no gate asked whether anything outside the graph answered to the identifier.

The same shape at both scales

A knowledge graph and a robot run fail the same way: several checks that look independent turn out to measure one thing, so a defect orthogonal to that thing passes every one of them. The response in Chapter 1 §4 was to add a check that leaves the graph and to record what was not compared rather than issue a certificate. The response here is the same move applied to execution: three measurements that are constructed to be independent, and a residual recorded in each rather than a single verdict.

tacat:notCompared from Chapter 1 and the three middle verdicts here are the same object: a statement of what the system did not establish, carried alongside what it did. That is the one idea running through all eight chapters.

6. What this does not establish

Be able to say this out loud
  • The example is constructed. I have never seen a LARA run. Plate 7 is built to make the three verdicts legible, which means it is built to make my case — the honest test is whether a real run produces three genuinely different decompositions, and I cannot run that test.
  • Three verdicts are harder to act on than one. That is a real cost and not a presentational one. An operator at 2 a.m. wants "continue or abort". The defence is that the single answer was never available — it was manufactured by discarding two of three facts — but a system that is right and unusable has lost to one that is approximate and used.
  • The independence claim is untested. Everything above depends on the three decompositions being genuinely different partitions. Nothing I have built demonstrates that yet. It is stated as an experiment in Chapter 8 §4 rather than claimed as a result.
