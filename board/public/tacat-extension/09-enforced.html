Chapter 9 · second series

Enforced, or merely represented

Chapter 1 argues that a floor must exist. This chapter argues something stronger and more useful: that a floor which no machine checks is not a floor. It is a field. The distinction is the single most transferable thing I have, because it is the one question that separates the parts of my own work that survived scrutiny from the parts that did not.

★★★ strongest transfer 10 min seven independent instances applied to my own work first
Provenance. Same hobby project as Chapters 6–8: single developer, no users, no peer review. What is different about this chapter is that the two tests in §1 were applied to my own modules before they were offered to anyone else, and §4 records what they found — including three cases where the answer was that a module fails its own standard. Those are not hypotheticals; they are named.

1. Two questions worth asking of any system

Both are cheap, both are answerable by reading code rather than documentation, and both routinely return an uncomfortable answer.

Test 1 · Enforced, or represented?

Does a machine reject a program that violates the discipline — or is the discipline a field that is stored, passed through, and never consulted?

A type checker that refuses to compile is enforcement. A JSON key named "floor" that nothing reads is representation wearing enforcement's clothes.

Test 2 · Measured, or synthesised?

Did data actually enter the calculation — or is the reported "validation" an analytic identity, computed from the same formulas it claims to confirm?

A check that cannot fail is not a check. If the only way to make it fail is to change the formula it is testing, it is a restatement.

Why these two and not others

Because they are the two ways a system can appear to have a property it does not have, and they are independent. A discipline can be genuinely enforced yet validated only against itself; a validation can be genuinely empirical while the discipline it validates is decorative. Passing one says nothing about the other. Almost everything I have written passes at most one.

2. The strongest form: making the wrong program unsayable

Enforcement has a weak form and a strong form, and the difference matters more than I expected when I started noticing it.

The weak form is a checker that runs and rejects. Something is submitted, something examines it, a verdict comes back. This is good, and it is what most static analysis is.

The strong form is a structure in which the violating program cannot be expressed at all — there is no checker because there is nothing to check. I have arrived at this three times from unrelated directions, which is why I now think it is a design move rather than a coincidence:

WhereThe violation that becomes unsayableHow
A diagnostic grammar A conclusion asserted without the evidence that would license it The production rule has no form in which the conclusion appears alone. There is no invalid parse to reject, because there is no invalid parse.
A scheduler's dispatch seam An observation leaking into the control path The dispatch callback's return type is a bare number. There is no channel through which a payload could travel, so there is nothing to sanitise.
A multi-repository pipeline Composing two stages whose environments are incompatible One container per repository. The wrong composition has no address space in which to be written. A boundary with no faithful encoding is correct information, not a bug.
The transferable form of it

When a rule matters, try to spend the design effort on making its violation inexpressible rather than on detecting it. Detection is a runtime cost you pay forever and a coverage question you can never fully close; inexpressibility is paid once, at the point where the type, the grammar production, or the process boundary is chosen. The three cases above cost nothing extra at runtime, and none of them can be circumvented by a caller who forgets to call the checker.

3. The check that failed, and why it is the best evidence here

Test 2 has a corollary that took me a long time to act on: a validation suite that has never rejected anything is indistinguishable from a validation suite that cannot. The only way to tell them apart is to find a case where the suite refused to certify something — and the most useful such case is one where the thing it refused to certify was mine.

One of the modules states a theorem about the optimal utilisation of a compute slot. The original form maximised a ratio: total progress divided by tick, cost and a strictly convex utilisation penalty. A numerical check was written against it. The check failed, and the reason it failed was not a bug in the check:

What the failing check established recorded in the results file, not only in prose

With the numerator constant in the variable being optimised and the denominator strictly convex, the ratio is strictly decreasing. Its maximum is therefore at the boundary, and the first-order condition has no interior root at all. The original proof's assertion of a "strictly concave numerator" was wrong — the numerator was constant.

The theorem was rewritten to a net-yield objective — a strictly concave benefit term minus a strictly convex cost term — which does have a unique interior maximiser at the point where the marginal benefit and marginal cost balance. The correction was then propagated into the implementing code, so the code and the paper now state the same theorem.

The failure is preserved in the machine-readable results alongside the passing checks, in a field that records what the earlier form was and why it broke, rather than being quietly deleted once fixed. The project's own gloss on this is the sentence I would defend in an interview:

Stated by the system about itself

"We report this because it illustrates the intended use of the validation suite: a machine check that refuses to certify a false claim is doing its job."

The same discipline appears in a second form, which is the one I would actually recommend copying: negative controls in the test suite. Rather than asserting only that a conforming implementation passes, the suite constructs three deliberately violating implementations — one that reads without advancing its counter, one whose history line decreases, one that constructs and commits in the same instant — and asserts that each is detected. The header states the reasoning outright: a pass on the conforming runtime is meaningful only because purpose-built violating runtimes are caught.

The direct application to this repository

G10 is a negative control — and it is the only one in the submission. Somebody deliberately broke every external identifier and re-ran the gates to see whether anything noticed. Nothing did. That experiment is worth more than the nine passing competency questions combined, because it is the only evidence in the repository about what the gates cannot see. The proposal that follows from this chapter is not a new gate: it is that every gate worth having should ship with the corruption that it catches, committed beside it and run in CI, so that "the suite is green" carries information.

4. Applying both tests to my own work

Twenty artefacts, read over the last several days with these two questions held fixed. The result is not flattering and that is the point of including it.

enforced Seven modules reject violating input structurally rather than storing a field: a type checker where an unfloored value is not typeable; a grammar with no unsupported-conclusion production; a dispatch seam with no payload channel; a container boundary with no shared address space; a test suite with negative controls; and two theorem-backed interfaces that restrict what a caller may say rather than validating what it did say.

That is the same common-mode failure as G10, committed by me, in a module written to demonstrate the fix for it.

The honest reading of that table

One in twenty. If the argument of this chapter is right, then by its own standard most of my own work does not meet it — and the parts that do are the recent ones, written after I started asking. I would rather state that than present the seven enforcing modules without the denominator. The reason the chapter is still worth reading is that the test is transferable even though my hit rate is poor: it costs a few minutes per artefact and it found five defects in work I believed was sound.

5. Where this argument is weak

Objections I cannot answer

Enforcement can be theatre too. A type that makes one violation unsayable often leaves a neighbouring one wide open, and the confidence from the first can mask the second. "Unsayable" is always relative to a specific violation, never general.

The measured/synthesised test has no formal statement. I apply it by reading and judging. Two people could disagree about a given case and I have no procedure for settling it.

One brute-forced case is not a proof. The artefact I called "measured" checks its equivalence at four tasks and four slots. That is a genuine check of a general claim at a single small instance — worth saying as "checked at n=4", not as "verified".

What survives them

The negative-control proposal in §3 does not depend on any of this being right. Shipping the corruption beside the gate is ordinary engineering with an ordinary justification, and G10 already demonstrates its value in this repository specifically.

The distinction in §1 is useful even if my classifications are wrong, because it is a question to ask rather than a verdict to accept. Its value showed up as five defects found, not as a taxonomy.

6. What I would actually propose from this

One change, small, and independently justified

For each automated gate in the submission, commit the input that the gate is supposed to reject, and assert in CI that it is rejected. G10 already exists as a one-off script; the proposal is to make it a permanent test rather than a finding in a document. The cost is roughly an hour. The benefit is that from then on, a green suite means "the gates caught the things we know they should catch", which is a different and much stronger statement than "nothing we tried happened to fail".

This is the same move as every other proposal in the first series, pointed at the test suite rather than at the graph: compare against something you do not own. A gate checked only against inputs it was designed to pass is checking itself.
