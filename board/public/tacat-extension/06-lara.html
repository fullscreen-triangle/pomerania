Chapter 6 · second series

The same three parts

The first five chapters proposed changes to the submission. These three describe something else: an operating system I have been building alone, as a hobby, for a while. It turns out to have the same three-part spine LARA has — a process language, an orchestrator, a scheduler — arrived at from a different direction and for a different reason. This chapter is about what that correspondence is worth, and what it is not.

★★ different register 10 min CoRDI 2023 · footnote 2 not part of the submission at all
Provenance, and a stronger warning than the earlier chapters carry. Chapters 1–5 proposed small changes to a repository that exists. This chapter and the two after it describe a personal project — one developer, no users, no publications, no reviewers. Nothing in it has been validated by anyone else. It is included because the correspondence with LARA's architecture is specific enough to be checkable, and because Chapter 8 states exactly how much of it runs today rather than leaving that to be discovered. If the correspondence turns out to be superficial, that is the answer, and Chapter 8 §4 says what would show it.

1. What LARA is, from its own paper

Doerr, Maak, Menke and Bornscheuer, The RDM System LARA — semantics through automation from bottom up, CoRDI 2023 (10.52825/CoRDI.v1i.359). The workflow section describes a three-stage pipeline:

CoRDI 2023 §1.3, quoted

"Processes can be formulated in the pythonLab process description language [4], which is interpreted by the LabOrchestrator [5]. The LabOrchestrator orchestrates the whole experiment, executed by machines and humans (also in collaboration between humans and machines). Which parts of processes are executed by which entity (machine or human) is described in the pythonLab procedures […] For the scheduling of all the experimental steps the LabScheduler[6] can be used. All data is collected in the LARA databases and automatically semantically enriched, using modular ontologies."

Three named components with three separate repositories, three separate roles, and — this is the part worth noticing — a division of labour that nothing in the paper forces. A process language could have carried its own execution. Scheduling could have lived inside the orchestrator. They are separate because the roles are genuinely different.

2. What I built, and the order I built it in

I did not set out to build a lab system. I set out from a question about individuation — the same question that generates the floor result in Chapter 1 — and the software followed from needing somewhere to run the resulting DSLs. The spine came out like this:

RoleLARAMineWhy the role exists at all
Process description pythonLab turbulance, plus a DSL per module A protocol is a program. Writing it as prose and executing it by hand loses the structure that makes it checkable.
Orchestration LabOrchestrator kwasa-kwasa Something has to decide what a step means and who performs it — machine, human, or neither yet.
Scheduling LabScheduler the scheduler module Steps compete for a shared resource in time. Ordering is a separate problem from meaning.
Semantic enrichment modular ontologies, SPARQL endpoint purpose — a context graph with a value function Not everything produced is worth keeping. Something has to decide what is retained and what is regenerable.
The claim, stated at its actual strength

Two people working on unrelated problems partitioned the same job the same way at the top level. That is weak evidence that the partition is real rather than an artefact of either project — the kind of evidence that would be worth nothing on its own and is worth something when the two derivations share no source. It is not evidence that my implementation is any good, and it is not a claim that the fourth row above is equivalent to a SPARQL endpoint. It is not.

3. Where the two designs diverge, and why that is the interesting part

A correspondence that went all the way down would be suspicious — it would mean I had reinvented LARA badly. The designs diverge at one specific place, and the divergence is the whole reason these chapters exist.

In LARA, the orchestrator interprets, the scheduler orders, and the databases record. Each stage produces something the next consumes. The pipeline is a line. In what I have been building, the three roles do not form a line: they are three independent measurements of the same task, each with its own decomposition into subtasks, each producing its own verdict, and the system deliberately never merges them into one number.

4. The footnote

The CoRDI paper carries one footnote on page 2, attached to the sentence about human intervention:

CoRDI 2023, footnote 2

"Modification of a running process is currently under development"

The sentence it hangs off says humans can always pause, interrupt or resume a running process. The footnote concedes that changing one is harder. That is not a small gap and it is not an implementation detail — it is the hardest problem in the design, and I think the reason it is hard is structural rather than incidental.

Why modifying a running process is hard, stated as a design problem

Pause, interrupt and resume are all operations on a process whose plan does not change. The state is frozen and restored. Modification is different: the plan changes while some of it has already executed, which means the system must answer a question it never had to answer before — is the work already done still valid under the new plan? Three sub-questions hide in that, and they come apart:

  • Does the completed work still support the goal? A wash step performed under the old plan may be irrelevant under the new one — not wrong, just no longer load-bearing.
  • Is the run still on course to finish? Adding steps mid-run can push a plate past the point where the remaining time or reagent makes completion possible.
  • Is the material already produced worth keeping? Some of it can be regenerated cheaply; some is a consumed sample that cannot.

A single "can we modify this?" gate has to collapse those three into one answer. They do not collapse — a run can be simultaneously still-necessary, no-longer-sufficient, and holding material that is worth keeping regardless.

That is the shape the three-verdict architecture was built for, arrived at from a wholly different problem. Chapter 7 works it through on a concrete screening run.

5. What this chapter does not establish

Be able to say this out loud
  • An architectural correspondence is not a working integration. Nothing I have built speaks SiLA, drives a Fanuc arm, or has been near a plate reader. The correspondence is at the level of how the problem is partitioned, which is the cheapest level to agree at.
  • I may be reading the footnote too generously. "Currently under development" may mean the design is settled and the code is being written, in which case my analysis of why it is hard is a solution to someone else's solved problem. The right move is to ask what makes it hard there before asserting what makes it hard in general — and that question is worth more than this chapter.
  • LARA is deployed and mine is not. A system running real screening on real hardware with real users has passed a test mine has not been submitted to. Any comparison that forgets that is not worth making.
